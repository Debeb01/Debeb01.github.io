Case Study · Senior Capstone

UAV Life Jacket Delivery System

KENNESAW STATE UNIVERSITY AERO LAB
Design · Build · Test · Analyze

A servo-actuated payload release for multirotor UAV drowning response

I designed, 3D printed, flight-tested, and structurally analyzed a lightweight release mechanism that lets an Aurelia X6 Standard multirotor carry a life jacket and drop it on command. The mechanism was designed in SOLIDWORKS and refined over five iterations, and I used a nonlinear finite element study to find its carrying capacity.

01

The Problem

Drowning takes more than 40 lives every hour worldwide, and over 85% of drowning deaths are preventable. Submersion time is the strongest factor in survival, so getting flotation to a victim quickly matters most. Fixed CCTV only covers what it can see, and throw lines and life rings are hard to throw accurately and are often not on site. A UAV is mobile, needs no infrastructure, and can carry a flotation device to a victim in a remote or hard-to-reach area. The UAVs built for this job are each specialized for a single flotation device, which limits what they can carry.

The engineering problem: release a life jacket from a multirotor on command, without it snagging on the aircraft, using a mechanism light enough that it doesn’t eat the UAV’s payload capacity.

02

The CAD Architecture

The final assembly has four functional groups, all modeled in SOLIDWORKS:

  • Payload bay hooks and mount (ABS, 3D printed): hexagonal pegs locate the hooks in the mount, so gravity keeps them seated until a part fractures. I shortened the hooks in the final iteration because the servo and linkage are heavier than the first concept’s release, and long hooks would have lowered the aircraft’s center of gravity.
  • Mechanism mount (I-beam): the structural spine that carries the servo and latch. An I-beam gave the stiffness-to-weight ratio the filament budget required.
  • MG996R torque servo with 25-tooth horn: chosen for the torque needed to hold the jacket without overloading the servo. I oriented it so the payload load runs parallel to the servo’s rotational axis, which keeps the load off the servo.
  • Linkage bar and latch: the jacket rests on the latch until the servo swings the linkage and releases it.

Design for Manufacture & Prototyping Progression

I treated every iteration as a design review against three questions: does it stay under 40 g of filament, will it release within 0.5 s, and will it hold 3 kg without fracturing? Five iterations across two different concepts got me to the final design.

ConceptIterationWhat the review foundWhat I changed
E-flite servoless payload release1Over the 40 g limit. Heat-set inserts would work loose under constant, turbulent load. The unfilleted wire hole was a fragile section.Removed material from the wings and replaced heat-set inserts with embedded nuts.

Print parameters. I printed the final parts on a Stratasys F170 in ABS with a cubic infill at 20% density. I picked those parameters from published work on how infill density and pattern change the yield stress of printed ABS (Seshaiah et al., 2024), because that settings choice sets the strength I design against. I used the yield stress of ABS at that infill (20.1 MPa) rather than the solid-material value, then applied a 1.5 factor of safety to get a 13.4 MPa allowable stress for the printed parts. ABS was the only filament available to me for this project, so I didn’t compare other materials.

The test was simple on purpose: hang the life jacket on the mechanism, press the switch, and time the release. It released inside 0.5 s with no stall or snag, and again on two repeat trials, which is what qualified iteration 3 as the working prototype.

04

Constraints, Loading & Results

  • Constraints: payload bay hooks fixed in place. I left the UAV payload bay out of the model to keep the solve time reasonable. Contacts between the latch and mount and between the bay mount and hooks were included.
  • Loading: a load applied uniformly to the latch section that carries the jacket, stepped from 0.5 kg to 50 kg in 0.5 kg increments (100 steps).
  • Factor of safety: 1.5 applied to each part’s yield stress, giving the allowable stress, since aerospace practice typically uses 1.5–2.
  • Method: I placed a probe on each load-bearing part at every step, recorded the maximum stress, and read off the load where each curve crosses its allowable-stress line.

What the analysis told me: my 25 kg hypothesis was not supported. The mechanism mount is the weak link, and the highest stress was at its corner between the attachment points and the servo, where the servo’s weight and the sharp corner combine with the payload load. The likely cause is the lopsided layout of the mechanism, which makes the mount take the brunt of the load. The FEA ran on the final design after it had passed the lab test, so it didn’t change this build. It suggests the next iteration should round that corner with a fillet and balance the layout. The capacity is still above what this UAV can lift: the Aurelia X6 Standard carries 5 kg, which leaves 4.85 kg after the mechanism’s 150 g.
05

Nonlinear FEA: Why and What It Showed

Why nonlinear. A linear static study assumes small deflections and a load whose direction and size never change. That doesn’t hold here: the mount and latch deflect enough that the direction of the load shifts as the part it acts on deflects, the load magnitude changes with each step, and the strains are large. For those cases SolidWorks recommends a nonlinear study, so I used one even though most of the literature I reviewed used static studies. I chose the Direct solver over FFEPlus because it is more accurate with parts of differing materials.

Setup. Load applied through a 100-step force multiplier curve (0.5 kg × step number), contact sets between touching parts, a fixed payload bay interface, and aluminum alloy and cubic-20% ABS assigned per part. Material behavior was treated as linear elastic, so the nonlinearity comes from the geometry, contact, and load stepping, not from plastic yield.
